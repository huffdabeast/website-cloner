The last decade of AI has been defined by language models that can reason, write code and solve increasingly complex problems. Engineering in the physical world is still hard for them. A design that reads well in text may not be geometrically accurate, physically feasible or manufacturable: engineering means keeping spatial relationships and constraints consistent across thousands of interconnected components, and language is an inefficient medium for that kind of reasoning.

We built SKY-001 to explore a fundamentally different approach: planning directly in the visual world. SKY-001 is a spatial world model that breaks a complex system into manageable parts and keeps them consistent across hundreds or thousands of construction steps. Unlike world models that generate environments to look at, explore or act in, it generates designs along with the sequences that build them. We believe world models can provide the spatial understanding and long-horizon planning that language models lack, and in turn unlock a new generation of physical agents.

In this post we show SKY-001 on two tasks. In CAD, it turns an image of an engineered product into a hierarchy of assemblies and parts, with the sketches and operations that build each part. In BIM, it turns a building outline into a layout of units, and each unit into its rooms. In both, it matches or exceeds frontier models in a fraction of the time and cost.

Pipeline Overview

Engineers rarely design a complex system in one flat pass. An engineered product such as an engine can break down into hundreds of parts across several levels, many nested inside others and hidden from the outside, and each part has to fit its neighbours. So engineers work level by level: the overall design, then its assemblies, then the individual parts and how to build them, with each level guiding the next.

SKY-001 follows the same order, in two stages. The Hierarchical Composer generates the target design and unfolds it level by level into a hierarchy of elements. The Construction World Model then predicts the visual sequence that builds each element. Together they produce a visual design plan.

This structure is what makes long rollouts tractable. A flat model would have to generate every part of a design, and every step that builds it, in one long chain, where each decision depends on all the others and errors accumulate over thousands of steps. Hierarchy breaks that chain. Once a parent is fixed, the rollouts of the elements can run in parallel, the way an engineering team divides the work, and the number of sequential steps grows with the depth of the design rather than its size. Each level also hands the one below an explicit subgoal, so a decision about a single bolt stays consistent with the whole engine, even hundreds of steps later.

CAD · Image to Part Tree

An industrial asset is more than a single mesh with regions labeled as parts. It is a hierarchy of components, and recognizing that these components should exist is not the same as generating their geometry or planning how they fit together. In CAD, the composer starts from a single image. It first generates a rough 3D shape of the whole object, enough to decide what it is made of, then takes it apart: it splits the object into its main assemblies, splits each assembly again, and keeps going until every piece is a single part.

The 3D shape the composer first generates from the image is only a rough idea of the design. It is good enough to decide what the parts are and where they go, but its surfaces are coarse, and everything hidden inside is missing, so splitting it gives rough, open pieces: a gear stack behind the timing cover, or a sleeve buried inside a housing, comes out as an open shell. Once the splitting stops, a refiner turns each leaf into a precise part, filling in what was hidden and adding the fine detail the rough shape never had.

BIM · Building Outline to Floor Plan

A building is a hierarchy too: each storey holds apartments and the shared circulation between them, and each apartment holds its rooms. In BIM, the composer starts from what an architect can write down: the envelope of the storey, with the thickness of every wall and its open edges, and a short brief with the programme, the net area and each room's size and rough position. From the brief we also generate a simple layout sketch, one box per room at the position and size the brief gives, so the model knows where rooms go, not only what they are. The composer generates the whole plan at once, down to doors, windows and fixtures, and checks it against the brief; a large storey is then split into parts that are each planned again and joined back.

Below is an example, one storey of an apartment building, about 29 metres across. From one envelope and one brief, SKY-001 places its walls and circulation and divides the floor into three apartments around a shared corridor, stairs and lifts.

CAD · Part to Sketches and Operations

CAD parts are rarely created through one uninterrupted sketch. Designers move through a sequence of intermediate 3D states: a profile becomes an extrusion, new sketches add or remove material, and later operations, like a cut that makes a hole, refine the result. Each state is a subgoal, so the construction of a complex part becomes a series of smaller design problems. SKY-001 works the same way: it plans the intermediate 3D states of a part, and between each pair of consecutive states it draws the 2D sketch that turns one into the next.

The films below show this on four assemblies, one part at a time: the view zooms onto the part, then its sketches and 3D states are drawn in turn. BIM · Floor Plan to Construction Sequence

In BIM, an architect does not draw a floor in one stroke either. The exterior walls go down first, then the walls between apartments and the shared corridors, then each apartment is filled in room by room, with its doors, windows and fixtures. The construction world model learns this drawing order from step-by-step plan histories, recorded or reconstructed. Given the finished plan from the composer, it draws the floor one state at a time and decides for itself when the drawing is done; a whole floor rolls out in about a second. 2D Rollout · Drawing Each State

In both examples above, the drawing itself happens on a 2D canvas: in CAD, the sketch between two 3D states; in BIM, each state of the floor plan. The 2D world model draws these canvases with diffusion, in two passes. It first rolls out the whole sequence roughly, in a compressed latent space, and then refines it. The figure below follows one CAD sketch through both passes.

The compressed space comes from a Matryoshka-style autoencoder, which sums up each state as a sequence of codes. Training starts with a long sequence and drops codes from the end step by step while still asking for the same reconstruction, so the earliest codes have to hold the global variation between states, the overall layout, and the later ones only finer detail. The coarse rollout runs in this highly compressed space, using only the leading codes of each state, so its reconstruction error is relatively large, but it keeps the overall layout of each state. The refiner then redraws every state at full resolution, based on the coarse rollout, and brings back what the short code left out: sharp lines, exact corners and small features.

Training Data

Our CAD data comes from publicly available online datasets of real product assemblies. The raw files are not ready to train on: their assembly trees are inconsistent, faces overlap, geometry is duplicated, unrelated objects are mixed in, and parts carry no names.

To turn these files into training data, every asset goes through an extensive cleanup that rebuilds its hierarchy to follow standard engineering design practice, from the full assembly down to individual parts. Every node of that hierarchy then becomes a training example for the composer: it learns to split a whole product into its assemblies, and each assembly into its parts, down to a fastener that is barely visible in the full assembly. The construction world model also needs to see how each part was built, not only what it looks like. Some public datasets come with the 2D sketches and the full construction history. Where that history is missing, we recover it: engineering tools and LLM-based workflows reverse-engineer construction trajectories from the finished parts and verify them before they are used for training. The whole collection pipeline is efficient by design: once its output was checked by hand at the start, it runs with almost no human in the loop, which is what lets it scale. In all, the composer learns from more than 200,000 parts, and the construction world model from over 600,000 construction examples.

The resulting data is of very high quality, well beyond the state-of-the-art datasets available today, such as PartVerse, which is widely used for part decomposition. Counted in parts per object, PartVerse has a median of 6 and a 99th percentile of 33; our data has a median of 288 and a 99th percentile of 4,180. In the examples below, a BMX bicycle from PartVerse has 8 parts and a fat bike from ours has 988.

For BIM, the floor plans come from publicly available datasets of apartment plans and building models, and here too the raw data is not ready to train on: plans sit rotated in their frames, the same kind of room goes by many names, and many building models define no rooms at all. Every plan is straightened to its main wall axis, its rooms are mapped onto one shared set of room types, and it is redrawn as a labelled plan of rooms, walls, doors, windows and fixtures, paired with a brief written from the plan itself. Some sources record how each plan was drawn, step by step, and that history becomes the construction world model's training sequence. For the other plans, we reconstruct a drawing order. Synthetic plans, generated inside real building footprints, widen the set further. In all, the BIM models learn from about 35,000 real and synthetic floor plans, some two million drawing states.

In comparison to the state-of-the-art model GPT-6 Astra, SKY-001 achieves about a 50× speedup and costs about 150 times less per case, while matching its performance (79.7% IoU against 79.1%).

Two choices make this possible. The hierarchy turns one long construction program into short steps: SKY-001 plans the intermediate 3D states first, then the sketch between each pair. Latent compression keeps those steps light: the overall plan needs no fine detail, so it runs on compact latents, and a refiner adds the detail back afterwards.

BIM
Construction World Model

The BIM construction benchmark has 1,000 floor plans: about 350 whole floors at building level, from IFC building models, and 650 at apartment level. Each takes from about 20 to more than 200 drawing steps, around 70 on average, and the final floor plan is scored by IoU. On this benchmark, the construction world model builds each case in about a second, at a fraction of a cent. Compared with GPT-6 Astra, widely regarded as the strongest model for design, that is about 120 times faster and about 185 times cheaper per case. It also leads on IoU, 43.6% against 40.6% for GPT-6 Astra.

What Comes Next

We presented our framework in two domains, CAD and BIM, and our next steps start there. In CAD, sketch rollouts would become executable operations with an editable feature history, and text instructions could guide the design directly: a designer could ask to widen a bracket or move a shaft, and SKY-001 would update the neighboring parts that must change with it. In BIM, generated floor plans and their drawing sequences would become walls, doors, windows and rooms in a building model that architects can keep editing. Beyond CAD and BIM, the same approach, turning a high-level goal into a structured plan and carrying it through, extends to PCB design and other hardware, as a step toward Skyfall's longer-term vision of autonomous businesses.
